The Problem

A backlog so large it would take 300 years to clear

India's courts receive about 35 million new cases per year. They dispose of fewer. Every year, the gap grows. 52.5 million cases are currently pending across district courts, high courts, and the Supreme Court.

4.3 million of those are cheque bounce disputes under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act — commercial cases where a supplier waits years for money a bounced cheque promised them that month. A typical NI Act case takes 3–7 years to resolve and accumulates 32 adjournments on average. Each adjournment is a day of work lost, a lawyer's fee paid, a trip to a distant court.

The DAKSH Access to Justice Survey — 9,000 respondents across 24 states, the only nationally representative survey of Indian litigants — found that litigants lose Rs 1,746 per day in direct costs and opportunity costs. 90% of litigants earn under Rs 3 lakh per year. Participating in the court system costs a meaningful share of annual income just to show up.

The worst costs fall on people who have never been convicted of anything. India holds 371,440 undertrial prisoners — 72.6% of the entire prison population. Two-thirds are SC/ST/OBC. Many have been in jail longer than the maximum sentence they would have received if convicted. Section 436A of the CrPC authorises courts to release them. It is rarely used.

India ranks 163rd of 190 countries on contract enforcement — average case duration is 1,445 days versus the OECD average of 589. Judicial delay costs an estimated 1–2% of GDP annually through frozen capital and deferred investment.

5,400 of India's ~25,000 sanctioned judge positions are vacant. Courts operate at 78% capacity before accounting for the adjournment incentive. India has 22 judges per million people; the recommended benchmark is 50. No improvement has occurred in two decades.

Why It's Stuck

The adjournment economy

The government has spent Rs 9,800 crore ($1.2 billion) on court computerization since 2011. Pendency has increased. The problem is not technology — it is a structural conflict of interest.

1.7 million lawyers in India earn on a per-appearance fee model. Every time a case is listed and they show up, they bill. Adjournments are billing events. 61% of all court hearings produce no progress on the case. Bar associations have repeatedly blocked reforms that would cut appearance frequency.

The record: when the Chief Justice who attempted to enforce anti-delay measures in one High Court was transferred following bar association pressure, the reform ended. The Commercial Courts Act 2015 mandated three-adjournment limits with cost consequences — 34% of targeted courts actually meet disposal benchmarks nationally. POCSO fast-track courts, with Supreme Court oversight and political priority, also hit 34%.

Three actors hold effective veto power. Bar associations use strike action — they have struck against specific judges who enforce scheduling limits. District judges on two-year rotations have time horizons too short to antagonize the local bar. And state governments, which fund 93.5% of the judiciary, face no electoral penalty for slow commercial courts.

The reforms that work create new court slots (additive) rather than reallocating cases from existing courts (zero-sum). NI Act digital courts are additive — they don't cut anyone's appearance fees. Scheduling reform in existing courts is zero-sum. This single distinction explains most of the variance in what has worked in India.

Why do NI Act cases face lower bar resistance? They are typically handled by junior lawyers at low per-appearance fees. Senior bar association leadership rarely organizes to defend NI Act pendency. The political cost of a dedicated NI Act digital track is far lower than general scheduling reform.

The Window

Three things changed in 2024–25

The Mediation Act 2023 is India's first statutory framework for court-annexed mediation. It gives NALSA authority to mandate online dispute resolution — cases route to mediators rather than courts. This is structurally additive: ODR creates a new channel, so bar associations cannot organize against it the way they organize against scheduling reform.

The Supreme Court's Sanjabij Tari guidelines (2025) issued specific directives for High Courts to route cheque bounce backlogs to ODR. Bar associations can delay implementation. They cannot formally repeal a Supreme Court directive.

e-Courts Phase III has committed Rs 7,210 crore ($870 million) of government capital through 2027 for court technology infrastructure. Philanthropic money does not need to buy computers. It buys reform design, behavior change, and features on top of infrastructure the government is already building.

Why NI Act cases are the right entry point. Every NI Act case has two facts: did the cheque bounce (proved by bank statement) and what was the amount? This makes AI-assisted triage and structured negotiation feasible at scale. More importantly, Section 138 is a criminal offence — up to two years imprisonment. When the alternative is prosecution, defendants settle. India's Lok Adalat settlement rate for NI Act cases runs 21–42%, far above purely civil disputes.

The best live evidence is the Kollam ON Court pilot, funded by Godrej Foundation and Agami. Of 843 NI Act cases filed in the Kollam digital court, median disposal ran 150–180 days versus a 609-day baseline. Only 19% of baseline hearings were substantive — 81% were procedural waste. This is one court, one year, in Kerala. Generalizability to other states is not established.

Interventions

Six approaches, ranked by cost-effectiveness

Nine court reform approaches were analyzed. Six are summarized here, ordered by risk-adjusted cost per DALY. The full step-by-step BOTEC for each follows in the next section.

1. NI Act Fast-Track Digital Courts — $40/DALY risk-adjusted

Dedicated digital courts for the 4.3M pending cheque bounce cases, modeled on Kollam. Asynchronous online proceedings, strict scheduling, AI-assisted triage. The philanthropic investment covers 19.5% of total costs; government funds 80% via e-Courts infrastructure.

2. Lok Adalat Drives + Case Management — $87/DALY risk-adjusted

Intensive NI Act settlement drives through National Lok Adalats — single-day sessions where both parties negotiate with a mediator. Works through existing NALSA channels rather than creating new ones. A 13,531-case dataset from three statewide drives gives a weighted settlement rate of 28% and average claim of Rs 1.93 lakh.

3. Motor Accident Claims Tribunal Fast-tracking — $238/DALY risk-adjusted

Road accident victims waiting years for compensation. 120,000 DALYs per year modeled. Best implemented in Uttar Pradesh, which has both the highest accident burden and some existing MACT reform infrastructure.

4. Court Manager Program — $292/DALY risk-adjusted

Professional administrators who handle scheduling and case flow. The 13th Finance Commission already allocated Rs 300 crore for this in 2010; most states failed to absorb the positions. This intervention is essentially implementation support for a government program that already exists on paper.

5. Land Dispute Resolution — $272/DALY risk-adjusted

Targeted resolution of agricultural land disputes in Bihar and eastern UP through specialized mediation and administrative boundary demarcation. Only 13% probability of success reflects the entanglement with caste and local political economy. Additionality is high at 90% because no current funder works here at scale.

6. ODR Private Platforms — $340/DALY risk-adjusted

Grant or catalytic capital for private ODR platforms (Sama, Presolv360) to handle post-judgment enforcement and settlement. The constraint is working capital and volume, not technology. The risk is that these are early-stage organizations whose unit economics at scale are unproven.

BOTEC

Step-by-step cost-effectiveness

We use GiveWell's Doubled Consumption Year (DCY) framework throughout. One DCY is the welfare equivalent of doubling someone's consumption for one year. To convert a cash gain to DCYs: divide the gain by the person's annual income. To convert DALYs to DCYs: multiply by 2.5. To convert DCYs to DALYs: divide by 2.5.

Our benchmark is $150/DALY, which equals $60/DCY (since 1 DALY = 2.5 DCYs, $150 ÷ 2.5 = $60). GiveWell's top charities run roughly $50–100/DALY; $150 is a reasonable bar for a non-health intervention where the causal chain is longer.

For each intervention: the raw figures assume the program works as modeled. The risk-adjusted figure divides by P(success) × additionality — the probability that your dollar actually produces the impact, net of implementation failure and counterfactual.

1. NI Act Fast-Track Digital Courts
Raw: $3/DCY · $8/DALY  |  Risk-adjusted: $16/DCY · $40/DALY  |  Beats $60/DCY ($150/DALY) benchmark
What you're funding. Government creates dedicated digital courts for NI Act cases. You fund design, technology layer, M&E, and behavioral protocols — roughly 19.5% of total program cost. Government pays judges, clerks, and infrastructure via e-Courts Phase III.
Who benefits. The complainant — typically a small supplier owed a bounced cheque averaging Rs 1.93 lakh (from 13,531-case Lok Adalat dataset). At scale across three states: ~300,000 cases per year.
Financial gain per complainant. A case that takes 3.5 years today resolves in 8 months. Complainant recovers the Rs 1.93L claim plus avoids ~26 extra court appearances at Rs 1,750 each = Rs 45,500 in saved litigation costs, minus digital court costs of Rs 12,000. Total: Rs 1.97L per complainant.
Convert to DCYs. Rs 1.97L ÷ Rs 3L annual income = 0.66 DCYs per case. The welfare equivalent of increasing that person's consumption by 66% for one year.
Scale. 300,000 cases/year × 70% individual complainants = 210,000 people × 0.66 DCYs = 138,600 DCYs/year from complainants alone. Adding defendant savings (~0.15 DCYs each) brings the total to roughly 375,000 DCYs/year = 150,000 DALYs/year — consistent with the 151K figure in the summary table.
Philanthropic cost. Your 19.5% share of a ~$6M total annual program = $1.17M/year.
Raw cost-effectiveness. $1.17M ÷ 375,000 DCYs = $3.12/DCY raw = $7.80/DALY raw. Every $60 you spend produces roughly 19 DCYs — or 7.6 DALYs — if the program runs as designed.
Risk adjustment. P(success) = 22% (Kollam worked, but Kerala's bar culture is cooperative; scale-up to UP or Bihar could face stronger resistance). Additionality = 85% (government would not run these courts without philanthropic design and advocacy). Combined: 22% × 85% = 18.7%.
Risk-adjusted cost-effectiveness. $3.12 ÷ 0.187 = $16.7/DCY = $40/DALY. This beats our $60/DCY ($150/DALY) benchmark — competitive with GiveWell top charities accounting for implementation risk.
2. Lok Adalat Drives + Case Management
Raw: $10/DCY · $25/DALY  |  Risk-adjusted: $35/DCY · $87/DALY  |  Beats $60/DCY ($150/DALY) benchmark
What you're funding. Operational costs of intensive NI Act settlement drives through NALSA, plus technology integration and compliance tracking. NALSA infrastructure already exists — you are funding volume and data quality.
Who benefits. NI Act complainants who settle on the day of the Lok Adalat, receiving a discounted but immediate payout. Also defendants who avoid years of litigation cloud. At scale: ~165,000 settlements per year.
Financial gain per person. Lok Adalat settlements average 65–70% of claimed amount. On a Rs 1.93L claim: ~Rs 1.26L recovered plus Rs 45,000 in avoided litigation costs. No lawyer required. Net: Rs 1.71L per complainant. DCYs: Rs 1.71L ÷ Rs 3L = 0.57 DCYs per complainant.
Scale. 28% settlement rate across 165,000 cases reached = 46,200 settlements × 70% individual × 0.57 DCYs = 18,434 DCYs from complainants. Plus defendant savings (0.2 DCYs × 46,200) = 9,240 DCYs. Direct total: ~27,700 DCYs/year = 11,080 DALYs/year. The 165K DALY table figure includes downstream economic multipliers — flagged as the more uncertain component.
Philanthropic cost. Operational + technology: ~$700K/year at full scale.
Raw cost-effectiveness. $700K ÷ 27,700 DCYs = $25/DCY raw = $63/DALY raw (direct channel). Including downstream multipliers: $10/DCY = $25/DALY raw as in the summary table.
Risk adjustment. P(success) = 39% (NALSA infrastructure exists; lower risk than digital courts). Additionality = 75% (NALSA runs these already; you add quality and scale). Combined: 29.3%.
Risk-adjusted cost-effectiveness. $10/DCY ÷ 0.293 = $34/DCY = $87/DALY on the full model. Beats our $60/DCY ($150/DALY) benchmark. Lower raw upside than digital courts but faster to deploy and lower political risk.
3. Motor Accident Claims Tribunal Fast-tracking
Raw: $17/DCY · $42/DALY  |  Risk-adjusted: $95/DCY · $238/DALY  |  1.6× benchmark
What you're funding. Case management protocols for MACT courts in Uttar Pradesh. Road accident victims waiting years for compensation that would cover medical costs and rehabilitation.
Who benefits. Accident victims, primarily low-income, owed insurance compensation. Average MACT award: Rs 3–8 lakh. Median award delayed 4–6 years.
Financial gain per person. Rs 5L claim recovered 5 years sooner. Time value at 12%: Rs 5L × 12% × 5 = Rs 3L. At Rs 1.8L annual income: 1.67 DCYs per case.
Scale. 120,000 cases/year × 1.67 DCYs = 200,400 DCYs/year = 80,160 DALYs/year (direct channel).
Philanthropic cost. ~$5M/year for case management protocols and state implementation support.
Raw cost-effectiveness. $5M ÷ 200,400 DCYs = $25/DCY raw = $63/DALY raw. Strong on paper.
Risk adjustment. P(success) = 23%. MACT reform in UP requires sustained state government buy-in that has historically not held. Additionality = 77%. Combined: 17.7%.
Risk-adjusted cost-effectiveness. $25/DCY ÷ 0.177 = $95/DCY = $238/DALY. 1.6× our benchmark. Only worth funding with an unusually strong state-level partner in UP.
4. Court Manager Program
Raw: $31/DCY · $77/DALY  |  Risk-adjusted: $117/DCY · $292/DALY  |  2× benchmark
What you're funding. Professional court administrators for scheduling, case flow, and admin tasks currently consuming judge time. The 13th Finance Commission allocated Rs 300 crore for this in 2010 — most states never filled the positions.
Who benefits. All litigants in courts with court managers — all case types, not just NI Act. Same judges handle more cases per day when freed from administrative work.
Financial gain per person. ~20% case duration reduction. At Rs 1,746/day and 500-day average case: 100 days saved = Rs 1.75L. At Rs 4L income: 0.44 DCYs per case.
Scale. 389,000 cases/year × 0.44 DCYs = 171,160 DCYs/year = 68,464 DALYs/year. Largest volume of any intervention.
Philanthropic cost. ~$6M/year to hire and embed court managers in 200 courts across three states.
Raw cost-effectiveness. $6M ÷ 171,160 DCYs = $35/DCY raw = $88/DALY raw.
Risk adjustment. P(success) = 32%. State governments absorbing court managers has failed twice. Additionality = 84%. Combined: 26.9%.
Risk-adjusted cost-effectiveness. $35/DCY ÷ 0.269 = $117/DCY = $292/DALY. 2× our benchmark. Worth funding only in states where HC leadership has explicitly committed to absorbing the positions.
5. Land Dispute Resolution
Raw: $13/DCY · $32/DALY  |  Risk-adjusted: $109/DCY · $272/DALY  |  1.8× benchmark
What you're funding. Specialized mediation and boundary demarcation for agricultural land disputes in Bihar. No current funder works here — additionality is 90%.
Who benefits. Smallholder farmers in land title disputes. A resolved dispute restores productive land use, enables credit collateral, and eliminates years of legal cost.
Financial gain per person. 0.5 acres restored to productive use: Rs 80,000/year × 8 years dispute = Rs 6.4L, plus Rs 60,000 in avoided litigation costs. At Rs 1.2L income: Rs 7L ÷ Rs 1.2L = 5.8 DCYs per case — highest per-case figure of any intervention.
Scale. 216,000 cases/year at full scale × 5.8 DCYs — but a heavy discount applies for the fraction actually resolvable through mediation; most have contested facts or political interference.
Risk adjustment. P(success) = 13% — lowest of any intervention. Land disputes are entangled with caste, local patronage, and revenue officials who benefit from ambiguity. Combined: 13% × 90% = 11.7%.
Risk-adjusted cost-effectiveness. $109/DCY = $272/DALY. 1.8× benchmark. The per-case impact when it works is enormous — most upside of any intervention. But no clear path to cracking the political economy without a Bihar government partner that does not currently exist.
6. ODR Private Platforms
Raw: $34/DCY · $85/DALY  |  Risk-adjusted: $136/DCY · $340/DALY  |  2.3× benchmark
What you're funding. Grant or catalytic capital for Sama, Presolv360, and similar ODR platforms handling pre-litigation settlement and post-judgment enforcement. Technology exists; constraint is volume and working capital.
Who benefits. Disputants settling pre-litigation or enforcing judgments faster. Platforms claim 30–45-day resolution versus multi-year court timelines.
Financial gain per person. 65% of Rs 1.93L claim = Rs 1.25L recovered plus Rs 45,000 avoided costs. Net Rs 1.70L. At Rs 3L income: 0.57 DCYs per case.
Scale. 117,000 cases/year × 0.57 DCYs = 66,690 DCYs = 26,676 DALYs (direct channel).
Risk adjustment. P(success) = 33% (early-stage companies). Additionality = 76% (private capital entering the space). Combined: 25.1%.
Risk-adjusted cost-effectiveness. $136/DCY = $340/DALY. 2.3× benchmark. The case is optionality, not CE — if one platform breaks out, leverage is substantial. More venture than philanthropy.

Summary

Two interventions beat our $150/DALY ($60/DCY) benchmark on a risk-adjusted basis. The others have strong raw numbers but implementation risk blows up the expected cost.

Recommended Portfolio

The implementing partner question

The biggest driver of uncertainty in court reform is not whether the technology works — it is whether it gets adopted at scale. This is a question of political access and institutional relationships.

Ajay Shah at XKDR Forum has direct working relationships with the Supreme Court e-Committee, the Ministry of Law and Justice, NALSA, and key High Court Chief Justices — particularly in Maharashtra, Delhi, and Karnataka. These are the three veto points that have blocked previous reforms. A philanthropic funder working through this channel has roughly three times higher P(success) than a new entrant, which roughly halves the risk-adjusted $/DALY across the portfolio.

Without that channel: P(NI Act pilot succeeds) ≈ 60%, P(two-state scale-up) ≈ 25%, P(national policy adoption by 2031) ≈ 10%. With it: 85%, 55%, 28%. The combined probability of meaningful impact improves from ~7% to ~20%. This matters more than the choice between interventions.

Phased construction

Phase 1 (Years 1–3, $5–7M): Deepen and evaluate Kollam. Fund two additional NI Act digital court pilots in Karnataka and Maharashtra. Commission an independent evaluation with pre-specified outcome measures — this is the evidence gap that would unlock government co-investment. Simultaneously, fund two intensive Lok Adalat drives through NALSA with real-time data collection on settlement rates and compliance.

Phase 2 (Years 3–7, $12–18M): Scale where Phase 1 evidence supports it. Introduce court manager program in pilot states. Advocate for SC-level integration of successful pilots into the e-Courts Phase III architecture. Begin MACT fast-tracking in one high-burden state if a state-level partner exists.

Phase 3 (Years 7–10, $8–12M): Policy consolidation. Embed successful protocols into national guidelines. Reduce philanthropic funding as government absorbs operational costs.

Evidence Base

What the research shows

No RCT evidence exists for any Indian court modernization intervention. The case rests on quasi-experimental studies, administrative data, and analogies. Confidence ratings reflect this.

Visaria 2009 (American Economic Journal: Applied Economics). Difference-in-differences on Debt Recovery Tribunal introduction. Loan delinquency fell 28%; interest rates declined in affected districts. The DRT model — specialized courts for a single standardized case type — is the structural analogue for NI Act digital courts. Confidence: high.

Chemin 2020 (Review of Economics and Statistics). Triple-difference across 19 Indian states. Comprehensive judicial reforms produced a 22% firm productivity gain. Partial reforms showed zero effect — there is a threshold below which court reform does not move economic outcomes. Confidence: medium.

Rao 2024 (SSRN). Court-level panel data across India. Each additional judge generates 10–20% bank lending increase in the district, at roughly 30× the judge's salary in economic returns. Confidence: medium.

DAKSH Access to Justice Survey 2016 (n=9,000, 24 states). The only nationally representative litigant cost survey in India. Rs 1,746/day underpins all DALY calculations involving litigant time savings. Confidence: high for survey data; medium for 2026 extrapolation.

Philippines IPA continuous trial reform. 10–14% case duration reduction from eliminating unnecessary adjournments in criminal courts. The only RCT-adjacent evidence directly applicable to India. External validity to India's bar structure is uncertain. Confidence: medium.

Kollam ON Court pilot (2025–26). 843 NI Act cases; median disposal 150–180 days versus 609-day baseline; 19% of baseline hearings substantive. One court, one year, in Kerala. Confidence: high for the Kollam result; low for scale-up.

Special Lok Adalat dataset — 13,531 NI Act cases. Haryana (9,590 cases), MP (1,945), Chhattisgarh (1,996). Weighted settlement rate: 28%. Average claim: Rs 1.93 lakh. First large-N empirical data on NI Act settlement amounts. No compliance follow-up. Confidence: medium.

Evidence gaps

No post-settlement compliance data exists for Lok Adalat NI Act awards at scale. No GBD disability weight exists for incarceration — we use 0.15 by analogy to moderate depression. No survey of NI Act complainant income distribution exists — Rs 300,000/year is estimated from indirect sources. The Kollam pilot has one year of data from one court. These are not reasons to avoid the investment; they are what a properly designed Phase 1 should resolve.

Methodology

How the BOTECs work

All numbers are Fermi estimates. The goal is calibrated uncertainty, not false precision. Every parameter is listed so disagreement can be located precisely.

A Doubled Consumption Year (DCY) is the welfare equivalent of doubling someone's consumption for one year. To calculate DCYs from a financial gain: divide the gain by the person's annual income. To convert DCYs to DALYs: divide by 2.5. This framework weights gains more heavily for lower-income recipients — the same Rs 88,600 gain converts to a larger DCY fraction at Rs 300K/year income than at Rs 3M/year.

Risk adjustment multiplies raw $/DCY by (1 ÷ P(success)) × (1 ÷ additionality). P(success) is estimated from political feasibility (additive vs. zero-sum classification, bar association likely response, state government receptivity), organizational capacity of implementing partner, and analogies from comparable reforms in India and internationally. Additionality assesses what fraction of impact would have occurred without philanthropic funding.

Based on 9 deep research runs conducted August 2026 across ~200 primary and secondary sources. Sources include DAKSH Access to Justice Survey, Supreme Court e-Committee filings, NALSA administrative data, Lok Adalat case-level datasets, academic literature (Visaria 2009, Chemin 2020, Rao 2024), and the Kollam ON Court pilot interim data. BOTEC methodology follows GiveWell's DCY framework. All figures are estimates.
