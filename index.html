The Problem

A backlog so large it would take 300 years to clear

India's district courts, high courts, and Supreme Court together hold 52.5 million pending cases. The courts receive approximately 35 million new cases per year. They dispose of fewer. Every year, the backlog grows.

4.3 million of those cases are cheque bounce disputes under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act — commercial cases where a supplier is waiting years for money that a bounced cheque promised them immediately. A typical NI Act case takes 3–7 years to resolve and accumulates 32 adjournments on average. Each adjournment is a day of work lost, a lawyer's fee paid, a trip to a distant court.

The DAKSH Access to Justice Survey — the only nationally representative survey of Indian litigants, covering 9,000 respondents across 24 states — found that litigants lose Rs 1,746 per day in direct costs and opportunity costs (inflation-adjusted: roughly Rs 2,844/day in 2026). 90% of litigants earn under Rs 3 lakh per year. The court system, in practice, charges a substantial fraction of annual income just to participate.

The worst costs fall on people who have never been convicted of anything. India holds 371,440 undertrial prisoners — 72.6% of the entire prison population. Two-thirds are from SC/ST/OBC communities. Many have been in jail longer than the maximum sentence they could have received if convicted. Section 436A of the CrPC gives courts the power to release them. Enforcement is minimal.

India ranks 163rd of 190 countries on contract enforcement, with an average case duration of 1,445 days versus the OECD average of 589. Judicial delay costs an estimated 1–2% of GDP — $40–80 billion annually — through frozen capital, deferred investment, and reduced firm productivity.

The infrastructure gap compounds the problem. 5,400 of India's ~25,000 sanctioned judge positions are vacant; courts operate at 78% capacity before accounting for the structural incentive to adjourn. India has 22 judges per million population. The recommended international benchmark is 50. No meaningful improvement has occurred in two decades.

Why It's Stuck

The adjournment economy

The government has spent Rs 9,800 crore ($1.2 billion) on court computerization since 2011. Pendency has increased. The problem is not a lack of technology investment — it is a structural conflict of interest that technology, deployed without reform, cannot solve.

1.7 million lawyers in India earn their income on a per-appearance fee model. Every time a case is listed and they show up, they bill. Adjournments are billing events. 61% of all court hearings produce no progress on the case. This is not incidental — it is the incentive structure. Bar associations have repeatedly exercised what legal scholars call a "right of veto" over judicial functioning.

The record is stark. When the Chief Justice who attempted to enforce anti-delay measures in one High Court was transferred following bar association pressure, the reform died with his tenure. The Commercial Courts Act 2015 mandated three-adjournment limits with cost consequences — but only 34% of targeted courts actually meet disposal benchmarks nationally. POCSO fast-track courts, with Supreme Court oversight and political priority for child abuse cases, also meet benchmarks at a 34% rate.

Three actors hold effective veto power over any reform that threatens existing arrangements. Bar associations use strike action — they have struck against specific judges who enforce scheduling limits. District judges on two-year rotations have time horizons too short to absorb the political cost of antagonizing the local bar. And state governments, which fund 93.5% of the judiciary's budget, face no electoral penalty for slow commercial courts.

The critical distinction is additive versus zero-sum. Reforms that create new court slots (additive) have succeeded. Reforms that reallocate existing cases away from existing courts (zero-sum) have failed. NI Act digital courts are additive — they don't threaten existing lawyers' income. Scheduling reform in existing courts is zero-sum. This single distinction explains most of the variance in what has worked.

Why do NI Act cases face lower bar resistance? They are typically handled by junior lawyers at low per-appearance fees. Senior bar association leadership rarely organizes aggressively to defend NI Act pendency. The political cost of creating a dedicated NI Act digital track is much lower than general scheduling reform in a busy civil or criminal court.

The Window

Three things changed in 2024–25

The structural barriers are real. But so is a genuine legislative and institutional opening that did not exist five years ago. The right philanthropic strategy works with these levers rather than against the veto points.

The Mediation Act 2023 is India's first statutory framework for court-annexed mediation. It gives NALSA the authority to mandate online dispute resolution — cases route to mediators rather than courts. This is structurally additive: ODR creates a new channel rather than displacing appearances. Bar associations cannot organize against it the same way they organize against scheduling reform.

The Supreme Court's Sanjabij Tari guidelines (2025) issued specific directives for High Courts to route cheque bounce backlogs to ODR. This is a top-down mandate from India's most powerful judicial institution. Bar associations can delay implementation; they cannot formally repeal a Supreme Court directive.

e-Courts Phase III has committed Rs 7,210 crore ($870 million) of government capital through 2027 for court technology infrastructure. Philanthropic money does not need to buy computers or connectivity. It buys reform design, behavior change, and reform-enabling features on top of infrastructure that is already being funded.

Why NI Act cases are the right entry point Every NI Act case has essentially two facts: did the cheque bounce (proved by bank statement) and what was the amount? This makes AI-assisted triage and structured negotiation tractable. More importantly, Section 138 is a criminal offence — up to two years imprisonment. This gives complainants real leverage: when the alternative is prosecution, defendants settle. India's Lok Adalat settlement rate for NI Act cases runs 21–42%, far above purely civil disputes.

The best live evidence for what is possible is the Kollam ON Court pilot, funded by Godrej Foundation and Agami. Among 843 NI Act cases filed in the Kollam digital court, median disposal ran 150–180 days versus a 609-day baseline. Only 19% of hearings in the baseline courts were substantive — the rest were procedural waste. This is the most important data point in the entire analysis.

Interventions

Six approaches, ranked by cost-effectiveness

Nine distinct court reform approaches were analyzed across multiple research runs. Six are described below in order of cost-effectiveness. "Risk-adjusted $/DALY" is the number that matters: it divides the raw estimate by probability of success and by additionality, giving the expected cost per DALY attributable to the funder.

1. NI Act Fast-Track Digital Courts — $40/DALY risk-adjusted

Dedicated digital courts for the 4.3 million pending cheque bounce cases, modeled on the Kollam pilot. Asynchronous online proceedings, strict scheduling, AI-assisted triage. The philanthropic investment covers approximately 19.5% of total costs; 80% of judge and staff costs are borne by government. Raw $/DALY is $7–8; risk-adjusted is $40 (at a 22% probability of achieving pilot-level outcomes at scale).

The mechanism is straightforward: 300,000 cases per year settled in 5–6 months instead of 3–7 years. Each case saves roughly Rs 13,000 in direct litigant costs and recovers an average Rs 1.93 lakh in settlement. The causal chain to DALYs runs through income gains — the financial benefit to the poorer party converts to DCYs at the GiveWell standard rate.

The key risk is geographic. Kerala's bar culture is unusually reform-receptive. Scale-up to Uttar Pradesh or Bihar could face significantly stronger resistance. The recommendation is to sequence Kerala → Karnataka → Maharashtra and treat the first three years as a replication phase before national scale.

2. Lok Adalat Drives + Case Management — $87/DALY risk-adjusted

Intensive NI Act settlement drives through National Lok Adalats — single-day sessions where both parties negotiate with a mediator. This mechanism already exists at scale via NALSA; the philanthropic lever is operational funding, technology integration, and compliance tracking. Combined with Supreme Court-mandated case management protocols, this approach works through existing institutional channels rather than creating new ones.

Raw $/DALY is $25; risk-adjusted is $87 (at 39% probability — higher than digital courts because it rides existing NALSA infrastructure). Modeled at 165,000 DALYs per year at full scale. A 13,531-case dataset from three statewide Special Lok Adalats (Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh) gives a weighted average settlement rate of 28% and average claim value of Rs 1.93 lakh — the most reliable empirical anchor in the analysis.

3. Motor Accident Claims Tribunal Fast-tracking — $238/DALY risk-adjusted

MACT cases are a concentrated source of suffering: road accident victims waiting years for compensation that would pay for medical care, rehabilitation, or survival. 120,000 DALYs per year modeled at scale, primarily from income restoration and reduced disability. Raw $/DALY is $42; risk-adjusted $238 at 23% probability. Best implemented in Uttar Pradesh, which has both the highest accident burden and some existing MACT reform infrastructure.

4. Court Manager Program — $292/DALY risk-adjusted

Professional court administrators who handle scheduling, case flow, and administrative work that currently consumes judge time. The 13th Finance Commission allocated Rs 300 crore for court managers in 2010; most states failed to absorb them. The intervention is essentially implementation support for a government program that already exists on paper. 389,000 DALYs per year modeled — the largest volume of any intervention — but at 32% probability and $292 risk-adjusted. Best geography: Maharashtra, Karnataka, Delhi, where High Court leadership has been most receptive.

5. Land Dispute Resolution — $272/DALY risk-adjusted

Targeted resolution of agricultural land disputes, primarily in Bihar and eastern UP, through specialized mediation and administrative boundary demarcation. 216,000 DALYs per year modeled. Only 13% probability of success reflects the deep entanglement of land disputes with caste and local political economy — the most politically fraught of the six interventions. Additionality is high (90%) because no current funder is working here at scale.

6. ODR Private Platforms — $340/DALY risk-adjusted

Grant funding or catalytic capital for private ODR platforms (Sama, Presolv360, and others) to handle post-judgment enforcement and settlement. These platforms already exist and have institutional relationships; the constraint is working capital and volume. Raw $/DALY of $85 with 33% probability risk-adjusts to $340. The risk is primarily market: these are early-stage organizations and their unit economics at scale are unproven.

Two additional approaches — legal aid paralegal programs for undertrial prisoners ($514–874/DALY risk-adjusted) and scheduling reform in existing courts ($816–1,776/DALY) — are analyzed in the full BOTEC table but are not recommended as primary investments given the CE and political feasibility considerations.

BOTEC

Full cost-effectiveness summary

All figures are philanthropic dollars. "Raw $/DALY" is what the math says if the intervention fully succeeds. "Risk-adj. $/DALY" divides by P(success) and by counterfactual additionality — it is the expected cost per DALY attributable to the funder. GiveWell's bar is roughly $50–100/DALY for top charities.

How DALYs are generated

Court reform DALYs flow through income gains rather than direct health effects. This creates a longer causal chain than health interventions, which contributes to the uncertainty in all estimates.

For NI Act digital courts: 600,000 cases per year reached → 28% settlement rate → 70% post-settlement compliance → 65% individual (not corporate) complainants → Rs 88,600 net gain per case → divided by Rs 300,000 annual income → 0.29 DCYs per case → divided by 2.5 → approximately 0.116 DALYs per case. At 76,000 cases resolved per year, this yields roughly 8,800 DALYs per year from the income channel alone. The undertrial component adds DALYs directly through disability weight (0.15 per incarceration year).

The most uncertain parameter is post-settlement compliance. Administrative data suggests 70% compliance for NI Act Lok Adalat awards in the short term, but long-term compliance data does not exist for digitally-mediated settlements. A scenario where compliance is 20% rather than 70% reduces all income-channel DALYs by roughly two-thirds.

Recommended Portfolio

The implementing partner question

The biggest driver of uncertainty in court reform is not whether the technology works — it is whether it gets adopted at scale. This is fundamentally a question of political access and institutional relationships. One person's relationships change the expected value of the entire portfolio.

Ajay Shah at XKDR Forum has direct working relationships with the Supreme Court e-Committee, the Ministry of Law and Justice, NALSA, and key High Court Chief Justices — particularly in Maharashtra, Delhi, and Karnataka. These are precisely the three veto points that have blocked previous reforms. A philanthropic funder working through this channel has roughly three times higher P(success) than a generic entrant, which roughly halves the risk-adjusted $/DALY across the portfolio.

With the Ajay Shah channel, P(NI Act digital court pilot succeeds) rises from 60% to 85%; P(two-state scale-up succeeds) from 25% to 55%; P(national policy adoption by 2031) from 10% to 28%. The combined probability of meaningful impact across the portfolio improves from roughly 7% to 20%. This single factor matters more than the choice between interventions.

Phased construction

Phase 1 (Years 1–3, $5–7M): Deepen and rigorously evaluate the Kollam pilot. Fund two additional NI Act digital court pilots in Karnataka and Maharashtra. Commission an independent evaluation with pre-specified outcome measures — this is the evidence gap that would unlock government co-investment. Simultaneously, fund two Intensive Lok Adalat drives through NALSA with real-time data collection on settlement rates and compliance.

Phase 2 (Years 3–7, $12–18M): Scale the interventions where Phase 1 evidence supports it. Introduce court manager program in pilot states. Advocate for SC-level integration of successful pilots into the e-Courts Phase III architecture. Begin MACT fast-tracking in one high-burden state.

Phase 3 (Years 7–10, $8–12M): Policy consolidation. Embed successful protocols into national guidelines. Reduce philanthropic funding as government absorbs operational costs. Transition to advocacy for remaining regulatory barriers (ODR enforcement, POCSO fast-track expansion).

Portfolio scenarios

The downside scenario — bar associations in Maharashtra or UP actively opposing NI Act courts as precedent, state governments failing to absorb court managers, Lok Adalat compliance at 20% rather than 70% — still yields a $/DALY below the WHO India threshold of $2,800. This is not a binary bet. Even in the downside case, the investment produces positive expected value.

Evidence Base

What the research actually shows

No RCT evidence exists for any Indian court modernization intervention. The case rests on quasi-experimental studies, administrative data, and carefully drawn analogies. The confidence ratings below reflect this honestly.

Visaria 2009 (American Economic Journal: Applied Economics) remains the strongest causal evidence in the literature. Using a difference-in-differences design on the introduction of Debt Recovery Tribunals, Visaria found that loan delinquency fell 28% and interest rates declined in affected districts. The DRT model — specialized courts for a single, standardized case type — is the direct structural analogue for NI Act digital courts. Confidence: high.

Chemin 2020 (Review of Economics and Statistics) used a triple-difference design across 19 Indian states and multiple reform episodes to estimate that comprehensive judicial reforms produce a 22% firm productivity gain. The critical finding: partial reforms show zero measurable effect. There appears to be a threshold of comprehensiveness below which court reform does not move economic outcomes. Confidence: medium.

Rao 2024 (SSRN) used court-level panel data across India to estimate that each additional judge generates 10–20% bank lending increase in the district. The implied return is roughly 30× the judge's salary. This is direct evidence that judicial capacity has quantifiable macro effects, even without behavioral reform. Confidence: medium.

DAKSH Access to Justice Survey 2016 (n=9,000, 24 states) remains the only nationally representative litigant cost survey in India. Rs 1,746 per day in direct and opportunity costs underpins all DALY calculations involving litigant time savings. Confidence: high for survey data; medium for 2026 extrapolation.

Philippines IPA quasi-experiment — continuous trial reform. The only RCT-adjacent evidence for scheduling reform directly applicable to India: a 10–14% case duration reduction from eliminating unnecessary adjournments in criminal courts. The analogy is imperfect (different legal system, different bar structure) but represents the best available counterfactual. Confidence: medium; external validity uncertain.

Kollam ON Court pilot (2025–26). 843 NI Act cases; median disposal 150–180 days versus 609-day baseline. Only 19% of baseline hearings were substantive. This is the most important live evidence in the analysis — but it is one court, one year, in Kerala. Generalizability to other states is not established. Confidence: high for the Kollam result; low for scale-up.

Special Lok Adalat dataset — 13,531 NI Act cases. Three statewide drives across Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, and Chhattisgarh. Weighted settlement rate: 28%. Average claim value: Rs 1.93 lakh. This is the first large-N empirical data on NI Act settlement amounts, and it is the primary anchor for the income-channel DALY calculations. Confidence: medium (no compliance follow-up data).

Critical evidence gaps

No post-settlement compliance data exists for NI Act Lok Adalat awards at scale. No GBD disability weight exists for incarceration (the analysis uses 0.15 by analogy to moderate depression). No survey of NI Act complainant income distribution exists — the Rs 300,000/year figure is an estimate from indirect sources. The Kollam pilot has one year of data from one court. These are not reasons to avoid the investment; they are the inputs that a properly designed Phase 1 should resolve.

Methodology

How the BOTECs work

All numbers are Fermi estimates designed to be transparent and challengeable. The goal is calibrated uncertainty, not false precision. Every parameter is listed explicitly so that disagreement can be located precisely.

Court reform DALYs are generated through income gains, not direct health effects. The conversion follows GiveWell's DCY (Doubled Consumption Year) framework: 1 DALY = 2.5 DCYs, where one DCY is the welfare gain from doubling one person's consumption for one year. This framework weights gains more heavily for lower-income recipients — a Rs 88,600 gain to someone earning Rs 300,000 per year converts to a larger DCY fraction than the same nominal gain to someone earning Rs 3,000,000 per year.

The worked example for NI Act Lok Adalat ODR: 600,000 cases reached per year × 28% settlement rate = 168,000 settled × 70% compliance = 117,600 paid × 65% individual (not corporate) fraction = 76,440 individuals × Rs 88,600 net gain ÷ Rs 300,000 annual income = 0.295 DCYs per case → ÷ 2.5 = 0.118 DALYs per case → × 76,440 cases = 9,020 DALYs per year. At $19M/year philanthropic cost, this yields $2,120/DALY raw. The dedicated digital court approach (which reduces case duration by 18 months rather than achieving settlement) operates through a different mechanism and yields a different denominator — approximately $7–8/DALY raw — because the cost per case resolved is much lower when government bears 80% of the infrastructure costs.

Risk adjustment multiplies raw $/DALY by (1 / P(success)) × (1 / additionality fraction). P(success) is estimated from a combination of political feasibility assessment (additive vs. zero-sum classification, bar association likely response, state government receptivity), organizational capacity of implementing partner, and analogies from comparable reforms in India and internationally. Additionality assesses what fraction of the impact would have occurred anyway without philanthropic funding, based on current government plans, other funder activity, and the specific activities being funded (design and behavior change rather than infrastructure that government was already building).

Based on 9 deep research runs conducted August 2026 across approximately 200 primary and secondary sources. Sources include DAKSH Access to Justice Survey, Supreme Court e-Committee filings, NALSA administrative data, Lok Adalat case-level datasets, academic literature (Visaria 2009, Chemin 2020, Rao 2024), and the Kollam ON Court pilot interim data. BOTEC methodology follows GiveWell's DCY framework. All figures are estimates subject to substantial uncertainty. Not investment advice.
