The Problem

A backlog so large it would take 300 years to clear

India's courts receive about 35 million new cases per year. They dispose of fewer. Every year, the gap grows. The damage is not abstract — it falls hardest on the poorest.

The scale

52.5 million pending cases across India's district courts, high courts, and Supreme Court. 4.3 million of them are cheque bounce (NI Act Section 138) cases — commercial disputes where a supplier is waiting years for payment that a bounced cheque promised them today.

The cost to litigants

The DAKSH survey of 9,000 litigants across 24 states found litigants lose Rs 1,746/day in direct costs and opportunity costs. A typical NI Act case takes 3–7 years, averaging 32 adjournments. Each adjournment is a day of work lost, a lawyer's fee paid, a trip to a distant court.

Who bears the burden

90% of litigants earn under Rs 3 lakh/year. 371,440 undertrial prisoners are awaiting trial — 72.6% of India's entire prison population — two-thirds of them from SC/ST/OBC communities. Many are in prison longer than the sentence they would have received if convicted.

The economic toll

Judicial delays cost an estimated 1–2% of GDP ($40–80B) annually through frozen capital, delayed contract enforcement, and lost productivity. India ranks 163rd of 190 countries on contract enforcement (1,445 days vs. OECD average of 589). This is a direct drag on investment and firm productivity.

The capacity gap

5,400 of India's ~25,000 sanctioned judge positions are vacant — courts operate at 78% capacity before accounting for adjournments. India has 22 judges per million population; the recommended benchmark is 50. No meaningful judge-to-case ratio improvement has occurred in 20 years.

The undertrial crisis

Of India's 574,000 prisoners, 371,440 are waiting for their trial to conclude. Many are in jail because they cannot afford bail — not because a court found them guilty. S.436A of the CrPC gives courts power to release them; enforcement is minimal. A day in jail is estimated at a 0.15 disability weight — equivalent in suffering to moderate depression.

Why It's Stuck

The adjournment economy

The government has spent Rs 9,800 crore ($1.2B) on court computerization since 2011. Pendency has increased. The problem is not a lack of technology — it's a structural conflict of interest.

The core structural problem 1.7 million lawyers in India earn their income on a per-appearance fee model: every time a case is listed and they show up, they bill. Adjournments are billing events. 61% of all court hearings produce no progress. This is not a bug — it is a feature of the incentive structure. Bar associations exercise what legal scholars call a "right of veto" over judicial functioning.

What bar associations have blocked

CJ Satish Chandra transfer

The Chief Justice who attempted to enforce anti-delay measures was transferred following bar association pressure. The reform died with his tenure.

Commercial Courts enforcement

The Commercial Courts Act 2015 mandated 3-adjournment limits with cost consequences. Only 34% of targeted courts actually meet disposal benchmarks — enforcement collapses in practice.

Fast-Track Court collapses

India has created and then allowed fast-track courts to lapse multiple times. The 13th Finance Commission allocated Rs 300 crore for court managers in 2010. Most states failed to absorb them — the infrastructure existed but the behavior did not change.

POCSO fast-track courts

Despite Supreme Court oversight and political priority for child abuse cases, only 34% of POCSO fast-track courts meet their disposal benchmarks.

The three actors with veto power

Bar associations

Strike action is the primary tool. Lawyers have struck against specific judges who enforce scheduling limits. The economic incentive to maintain the status quo is ~Rs 150/appearance × 32 adjournments per NI Act case = substantial income per case.

Judges on short rotations

District judges typically serve 2-year terms before transfer. The time horizon is too short to push through reforms that will make them unpopular with the local bar. There is no career incentive for systemic change.

State governments

93.5% of the judiciary's budget is state-funded. States face no political penalty for court delay — voters do not punish politicians for a slow commercial court. Absent political will, implementations stall.

The Window

Three things changed in 2024–25

The structural barriers are real — but so is a genuine legislative and institutional opening. The right strategy works with these new levers rather than against the existing veto points.

Mediation Act 2023

India's first statutory framework for court-annexed mediation. NALSA can now mandate online dispute resolution — cases go to mediators, not lawyers. This bypasses bar associations structurally, because ODR is additive rather than replacing lawyer appearances.

SC Sanjabij Tari guidelines (2025)

The Supreme Court issued specific directives for High Courts to route cheque bounce backlogs to ODR. This is a top-down mandate from India's most powerful judicial institution. Bar associations cannot formally block Supreme Court directives.

e-Courts Phase III ($870M)

Government capital of Rs 7,210 crore is available through 2027 for the technology layer. Philanthropic money does not need to buy computers — it buys design, behavior change, and reform-enabling features on top of this infrastructure.

The key insight: additive vs. zero-sum The reforms that have worked in India created new court slots (additive) rather than taking cases away from existing courts (zero-sum). NI Act fast-track digital courts are additive — they don't threaten existing lawyers' income. Scheduling reform in existing courts is zero-sum — it directly cuts appearance fees. This distinction drives the dramatic difference in political feasibility between interventions.

Why NI Act cases are the right entry point

Standardized cases

Every NI Act case has essentially two facts: did the cheque bounce? (bank statement proves it) and what was the amount? This makes AI-assisted triage and structured negotiation tractable in a way that complex civil or criminal cases are not.

Criminal leverage for settlement

Section 138 is a criminal offence — up to 2 years imprisonment. This gives complainants leverage. When the alternative is prosecution, defendants are motivated to settle. This is why India's Lok Adalat settlement rate for NI Act cases (28-42%) is far higher than for purely civil disputes.

Low bar resistance

NI Act cases are typically handled by junior lawyers at low fees. Senior bar association leadership rarely defends NI Act pendency aggressively. The political cost of creating a dedicated NI Act digital court is much lower than general scheduling reform.

How to read these numbers Raw $/DALY is what the math says if the intervention fully succeeds. Risk-adjusted $/DALY divides by P(success) and by additionality — it is the expected cost per DALY actually attributable to the funder, accounting for implementation risk and what would have happened anyway. How DALYs are generated — the causal chain

Court reform DALYs come through income gains (DCYs — Doubled Consumption Years), not direct health. This creates a longer causal chain than health interventions, which contributes to uncertainty.

Step 1: Case resolved faster

An NI Act case settled in 5 months vs. 3–7 years. Complainant recovers ~Rs 1.93 lakh in settlement. Both parties avoid ~32 adjournments × Rs 13,000/case in costs and lost wages = Rs 4.2 lakh in avoided total burden.

Step 2: Income gain → DCY

We count the net financial benefit to the poorer party. At Rs 300K/yr income and Rs 88,600 net gain, this equals 0.29 DCYs. We convert at 2.5 DCYs → 1 DALY, following GiveWell's consumption-doubling framework. This weights gains more heavily for poorer recipients.

Step 3: Scale → total DALYs

At 600K NI Act cases settled/yr × 28% settlement rate × 70% compliance × 0.192 DCYs/case ÷ 2.5 = 22,793–56,983 DALYs/yr per approach. Add undertrial release health DALYs directly (disability weight 0.15 × days freed).

Evidence Base

What the research actually shows

No RCT evidence exists for any Indian court modernization intervention. The case rests on quasi-experimental studies, administrative data, and careful analogies.

Strongest evidence

Visaria 2009 — Debt Recovery Tribunals (AEJ: Applied)

Diff-in-diff on DRT introduction. Loan delinquency reduced by 28%. Interest rates fell. Strongest causal evidence of any Indian judicial reform on economic outcomes. The DRT model (specialized courts for a specific case type) is the direct analogue for NI Act courts.

HIGH confidence

Chemin 2020 — Comprehensive reform → firm productivity (REStat)

Triple-diff across 19 Indian states and multiple reform episodes. Comprehensive judicial reforms → 22% firm productivity gain. Critical finding: partial reforms show zero effect — the threshold for impact requires genuine comprehensiveness.

MEDIUM confidence

Rao 2024 — Additional judges → credit expansion (SSRN)

Court-level panel data, all India. Each additional judge generates 10–20% bank lending increase and 30× salary in economic returns. Direct evidence that judicial capacity has quantifiable macro effects.

MEDIUM confidence

DAKSH Access to Justice Survey 2016 (n=9,000, 24 states)

The only nationally representative litigant cost survey in India. Rs 1,746/day in direct + opportunity costs (inflation-adjusted: Rs 2,844/day in 2026). Underpins all DALY calculations involving litigant time savings.

HIGH confidence

Philippines IPA RCT — Continuous trial reform

Quasi-experimental evaluation. 10–14% case duration reduction from continuous trial (no unnecessary adjournments) in criminal courts. The only RCT-adjacent evidence for scheduling reform that is directly applicable to India.

MEDIUM confidence

Key empirical data points

Kollam ON Court pilot (Godrej Foundation / Agami, 2025–26)

843 NI Act cases filed in Kollam digital court. Median disposal: 150–180 days vs. 609-day baseline. Only 19% of baseline hearings were substantive — 81% were procedural waste. The most important live pilot evidence.

HIGH confidence

Special Lok Adalat dataset — 13,531 NI Act cases (2026)

Three statewide Special Lok Adalats (Haryana: 9,590 cases, MP: 1,945 cases, Chhattisgarh: 1,996 cases). Weighted average settlement amount: Rs 1.93 lakh. Settlement rate: 21–42%, weighted average ~28%. First large-N empirical data on NI Act settlement amounts.

MEDIUM confidence

Commercial Courts Act — duration data

Commercial courts in major metros achieved 1,445 → 626 days case duration after 3-adjournment enforcement. 57% reduction — but only in best-case urban settings with motivated HC leadership. 34% of targeted courts actually meet benchmarks nationally.

MEDIUM confidence

NALSA UTRC administrative data (n=24,100)

74% bail grant rate when undertrial cases are properly identified by UTRC. The bottleneck is identification and presentation, not judicial decision-making. Key input for legal aid paralegal design.

MEDIUM-HIGH confidence

Critical evidence gaps

No RCT evidence exists This is the most significant limitation. No court modernization intervention in India has been evaluated with a randomized or quasi-experimental design with pre-specified outcome measures. The entire analysis is based on before-after data, administrative records, and cross-national analogies. P(success) estimates carry substantial uncertainty.
  • No post-settlement compliance data for NI Act Lok Adalat awards
  • No GBD disability weight for incarceration (we use 0.15 by analogy)
  • No survey of NI Act complainant income distribution
  • Kollam has 1-year data from 1 court — insufficient for confidence in scale-up
Methodology

How the BOTECs work

All numbers are Fermi estimates designed to be transparent and challengeable. The goal is calibrated uncertainty, not false precision.

Bottom line on uncertainty The pessimistic scenario ($378–2,746/DALY risk-adjusted for the full portfolio) still clears the WHO threshold for cost-effectiveness in India ($2,800/DALY). The base case ($40–87/DALY for the two lead interventions) is competitive with GiveWell top charities. The central question is not whether this is cost-effective — under almost all scenarios it clears the threshold — but whether the implementation risks can be managed. That is almost entirely a function of the implementing partner and their government relationships.
